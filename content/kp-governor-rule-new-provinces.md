On 11 October 2026, Pakistan's federal government imposed governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), suspending the elected provincial government led by Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI). The move came as a broader proposal to redraw Pakistan's four provinces into a larger number of smaller units gathered pace — a convergence that critics say points one way: weaker provinces, and a stronger centre.

## An elected government suspended

President Asif Ali Zardari declared an emergency and imposed governor's rule following a federal cabinet meeting chaired by Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, citing worsening law and order, militant attacks, and a breakdown of constitutional machinery. Chief Minister Sohail Afridi, his ministers, and advisers were required to vacate their offices; the powers of the KP Assembly were transferred to Parliament; and the province's governor, Faisal Karim Kundi of the Pakistan Peoples Party, took charge with an advisory council. Internet disruptions were reported in parts of Peshawar, and PTI condemned the decision.

The action followed a protest march that began on 4 October, led by Afridi, as part of the PTI's campaign demanding the release of its jailed founder, Imran Khan.

## A move to redraw the map

The suspension lands amid a serious debate in Islamabad over creating new provinces — a change widely expected to be pursued through a proposed 28th Constitutional Amendment. Proposals under discussion would divide the existing four provinces into roughly 12 to 15 units: in one draft, Punjab and Sindh into five each and KP and Balochistan into four each; in another, Balochistan into two and Punjab into three, with Karachi and Gwadar placed under direct federal control. Figures thrown around in public debate run higher, but the serious proposals cluster around a dozen or so.

## Autonomy, and who gains

The reorganisation would follow the 27th Amendment, passed in November 2025, which revised the command structure of the armed forces and the fiscal and administrative autonomy of the provinces — and which the KP government denounced as a "robbery of provincial autonomy" that undercut the devolution won under the landmark 18th Amendment.

Supporters present new provinces as devolution: bringing administration closer to citizens and breaking up an over-dominant Punjab. Critics — including Baloch and Pashtun nationalists — read it the other way: provinces that are individually smaller and weaker would shift real power back toward the federal centre, and toward the military establishment that dominates it. The Pakistan Peoples Party and the PML-N have publicly opposed the reorganisation; the PTI, long an advocate of new provinces, has been ambivalent.

Taken together — an elected provincial government dismissed, and a plan advancing to multiply and shrink the provinces — the two developments sharpen a question that recurs throughout Pakistan's history: where power finally rests, and how much of it the federation's units are to be left holding.
