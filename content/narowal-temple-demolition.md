A Hindu temple estimated to be between 100 and 125 years old, in Siraj village of Narowal district, Punjab — around 130 kilometres from Lahore — was demolished on 21 August. What brought it down is disputed: minority groups and local residents say it was deliberately cleared to expand an adjacent madrassa, while government officials say the ageing structure simply collapsed in torrential rain.

## Two accounts

According to local residents, minority organisations, and the Pakistan Masiha Millat Party (PMMP), the temple — which stood on roughly 1,000 square metres of land — was demolished by a group said to be linked to the Tehreek-i-Labbaik Pakistan (TLP), a banned far-right party, in order to fold its land into the neighbouring seminary.

Officials of the Evacuee Trust Property Board (ETPB), the state body that manages minority religious properties left after Partition, gave a different account. A senior ETPB official said the temple had collapsed because of heavy rain, adding that while it did not appear by name in the board's record books, it was indeed very old. Community members have questioned that explanation, noting that no neighbouring structures were reported damaged in the same period.

## An unimplemented order

The case for deliberate demolition rests partly on documents that predate the incident. The PMMP's chairman said the ETPB's own deputy administrator in Sialkot had issued an order on 29 January 2026 cancelling the seminary's lease rights and directing the eviction of illegal occupants and the sealing of the property — an order he said was never implemented. He also cited an April report attributed to a Narowal police officer indicating that authorities were already aware of security concerns around the site and the risk of damage to it.

A community leader who sits on the Narowal District Peace Committee said the district administration and the ETPB had failed to protect the site or remove its occupants, ultimately allowing the demolition to proceed. Hindu residents, he said, were now afraid to visit at all, with the madrassa still operating on the premises.

## A vanishing heritage

The demolition fits a long pattern of attrition against Hindu religious sites in Pakistan. In Narowal district alone, there were 45 Hindu temples at the time of Partition; over the decades almost all have fallen into disuse, disrepair, or encroachment. Across the country, the ETPB has repeatedly been criticised by minority advocates for failing to safeguard the properties in its custody, and at times for enabling their occupation.

Where temples have not decayed quietly, they have sometimes been attacked outright. In December 2020, a mob led by local clerics set fire to and demolished a temple in Karak, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, that the Supreme Court had earlier ordered restored. In August 2021, a mob vandalised and partly burned the Ganesh temple in Bhong, Rahim Yar Khan, after a blasphemy allegation against a Hindu child. Hindus, estimated to number several million and forming Pakistan's largest religious minority, are concentrated in Sindh, with only small and shrinking communities in Punjab's countryside.

## What comes next

The PMMP has asked the Punjab government to act against those responsible and to rebuild the temple; its chairman said he submitted a written application to the province's additional home secretary on 28 August. As of now, the two accounts — deliberate demolition or rain-driven collapse — remain formally unreconciled, and no reconstruction has begun. The community's central demand is a transparent investigation that establishes, on the record, what happened to the temple at Siraj.
