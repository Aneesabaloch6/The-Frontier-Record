The Committee to Protect Journalists (CPJ) has urged Pakistani authorities to withdraw sweeping new restrictions that bar television channels from airing live coverage of terrorist attacks and other vaguely defined "unpleasant" incidents.

## The directive

On 13 October, the Pakistan Electronic Media Regulatory Authority (PEMRA) ordered all satellite television channels to refrain from broadcasting, rebroadcasting, or airing live coverage of any "unfortunate" or "unpleasant" incident — including explosions, blasts, shootings, and terrorist attacks — according to a copy of the directive reviewed by CPJ.

The breadth of the wording is the problem. Terms like "unpleasant" and "unfortunate" have no fixed legal meaning, leaving it to the regulator to decide, after the fact, what a channel should not have shown. In practice, that pushes newsrooms toward reporting only what state and security agencies have cleared — turning a safety rationale into a mechanism of information control.

## CPJ's response

"Preventing journalists from independent reporting of major security incidents and requiring them to rely on information authorized by state and security agencies risks turning legitimate safety safeguards into a system of official information control and censorship," said Waliullah Rahmani, CPJ's Afghanistan and Pakistan representative.

"Pakistani authorities must rescind these sweeping restrictions and allow newsrooms to make responsible editorial decisions without fear of regulatory retaliation."

CPJ argued that journalists and media workers must be able to report breaking news and current affairs independently, free from fear of legal retaliation, and in the public interest.

## A tightening environment

The order is the latest turn in a steady narrowing of the space for independent reporting in Pakistan. It follows the Foreign Media Facilitation Guidelines earlier this year, which required official permission for foreign correspondents to report outside three cities, and comes amid repeated internet and communications blackouts — in Balochistan, in Pakistan-administered Kashmir, and most recently across Khyber Pakhtunkhwa under emergency rule. Taken together, the measures share a single logic: narrowing what the public, at home and abroad, is allowed to see of the state's own conduct during moments of crisis.
