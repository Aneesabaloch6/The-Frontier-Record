*The following is a comment by The Frontier Record.*

There is a familiar move in Pakistan's politics, and it was performed again on 11 October. When an elected government becomes inconvenient, it is not defeated at the ballot box — it is declared an emergency. On the advice of Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, President Asif Ali Zardari invoked Articles 232 and 234 of the Constitution to suspend the elected government of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), transfer the province's authority to a federally appointed governor, and hand the powers of its assembly to a Parliament the province's voters did not elect to run their affairs.

The official reason is terrorism. The proclamation speaks of attacks of "unprecedented violent intensity" and of a provincial government that failed to contain them; a federal minister went further, accusing that government of being "aligned with terrorists," while offering the public no evidence for the charge. The timing tells a plainer story. The emergency did not follow a single catastrophic attack. It followed a protest march — begun on 4 October and led by KP's own chief minister, Sohail Afridi — demanding the release of the jailed former prime minister, Imran Khan.

## The shape of the crackdown

What has happened since is not the careful, districted security response that a genuine terrorism emergency would produce. It is a province-wide political decapitation. Chief Minister Afridi has been detained; so, by his party's account, have its provincial president, the assembly speaker, and a string of ministers and lawmakers — several of them picked up before the proclamation was even signed, and some held at locations the authorities have not disclosed. Khan's own sisters have sat in preventive detention since late September. Schools and colleges were shut across Peshawar, Mardan, Swabi, and Nowshera. The internet monitor NetBlocks recorded a major disruption to connectivity across the province — the same digital darkness Pakistan has imposed on Balochistan, on Pakistan-administered Kashmir, and on every population it would rather the world did not watch too closely.

If the threat were really confined to a handful of districts, as the government's own designation of twelve special-administration districts implies, why suspend the elected government of the entire province? That question answers itself.

## Counter-terrorism as a cover

The pattern is the point. Across the territories The Frontier Record documents — Balochistan, the Pashtun belt — the counter-terrorism label has become the all-purpose justification for whatever the state wishes to do: to disappear activists, to ban peaceful movements, to switch off the internet, and now to dissolve an elected provincial government. A law written to fight armed militancy is turned, again and again, on unarmed political opponents. When "the writ of the state" is invoked, it rarely means the rule of law; it means the reach of the establishment.

Rights monitors have said as much. Amnesty International, responding to the emergency, warned that reports of arbitrary detentions and communications blackouts raised serious questions about proportionality, and reminded Islamabad that emergency powers "are not a blank cheque to crack down on human rights, silence dissent, or concentrate power." That is precisely what they are being used for.

## The audience abroad

None of this is unfolding in isolation. Pakistan's army chief, Field Marshal Asim Munir — the man who has presided over the long campaign against Khan's party and consolidated control of the armed forces — has in the same period been courted warmly in Washington. International indulgence of a military that governs from behind a civilian façade does not cause these crackdowns, but it lowers their cost. A general who is feted abroad has less reason to fear consequences at home, and the people of KP, Balochistan, and beyond are left to absorb the difference.

## What is actually at stake

Strip away the party colours and the geopolitics, and the principle is simple enough that it should not need defending. Millions of people in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa voted for a provincial government. That government has been removed not by them, but by the federal centre and the institution that stands behind it — on a pretext, under emergency powers, with its leaders in cells and its citizens cut off from the internet. Whatever one thinks of Imran Khan or his party, a federation that will not let a province keep the government it elected, and answers peaceful protest with preventive detention and blackout, is not managing a security crisis. It is managing its own people. That is the story KP has now joined — the one this site exists to record.
