A report published in October 2026 by INSIGHT UK, a British Hindu diaspora organisation, documents what it describes as decades of persecution of Pakistan's Hindu minority — a steep demographic decline, forced conversions of women and girls, repeated attacks on temples, and legal structures that place minorities at a permanent disadvantage. Titled *The Cost of Silence*, it is an advocacy report, and its sharper framing is its own; but several of its central claims are supported by independent bodies.

## A vanishing community

The report's starting point is a demographic one. Hindus made up roughly 14.6% of the population of the area that became Pakistan at the 1941 census, it says; today they are about 1.7% — a collapse it presents as evidence that the state has failed its largest religious minority. It points to structural disadvantages written into the system: constitutional provisions reserving the offices of president and prime minister for Muslims, the Enemy Property Act used to seize land left by Hindus who migrated, and the fact that Islamabad still has no functioning Hindu temple, construction of one having been halted in 2020 after protests.

## Forced conversions

The report's gravest section concerns the abduction, forced conversion, and forced marriage of Hindu and Christian women and girls — a practice campaigners estimate affects more than 1,000 a year, concentrated in Sindh. It names recurring cases and sites, including the Bharchundi Sharif shrine, which it says has been linked to scores of conversions of underage girls. These figures come from advocacy groups and are difficult to verify independently, but the underlying pattern is one the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan has itself flagged, calling forced conversions "a critical problem in Sindh."

## A documented timeline

The report assembles a long timeline of attacks — from Partition-era violence through the destruction of temples after the 1992 Babri demolition in India, the 2014 Larkana and 2020 Karak temple attacks, and a string of temple demolitions and desecrations in Karachi and Sindh through 2024. Many of these are independently recorded; the pattern tracks with incidents The Frontier Record has separately documented, including the recent demolition of a century-old temple in Narowal.

## The international record

Where the report is on firmest ground is in citing third parties. The US Commission on International Religious Freedom has long recommended Pakistan be designated a "Country of Particular Concern," and the US State Department has maintained that designation, including in its 2023 religious-freedom report, for "severe violations of religious freedom." In December 2022, the UK government sanctioned Mian Mithu — a cleric repeatedly accused of facilitating forced conversions — with an asset freeze and travel ban. Human Rights Watch has said an "entrenched bias" in Pakistan's criminal-justice system means those who commit violence in the name of blasphemy are almost never held to account.

The report's conclusion is a call to its own government and the wider international community: to treat the condition of Pakistan's Hindus, and of its religious minorities generally, as a matter deserving sustained scrutiny rather than silence.
