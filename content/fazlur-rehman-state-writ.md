Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam-Fazl (JUI-F) chief Maulana Fazlur Rehman has said that the writ of the state has collapsed across the Pashtun districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and much of Balochistan — among his sharpest public assessments yet of Pakistan's security crisis. Speaking to journalists at Parliament House in Islamabad, the veteran opposition leader described a breakdown of civil authority in the areas west of the Indus.

## "No writ west of the Indus"

Fazlur Rehman said there was, in his words, "no writ on the western bank of the Indus River" at present. In the KP districts of Lakki Marwat, Bannu and Tank, he claimed, the police chain of command had broken down: officers had formed their own committees, set up roadblocks, and no longer followed the orders of their District Police Officers or Deputy Inspectors General. Businesses had shut down and residents were migrating in large numbers, he added, while the government looked away.

By his account, the collapse of authority has gone further still. He cited an episode he attributed to Hangu, in which — he said — militants surrounded a police post and released the officers only after they had surrendered their weapons, later even handing them money.

## Balochistan: cities held, villages abandoned

On Balochistan, Fazlur Rehman said the army effectively secured only the cities, leaving rural areas to armed groups. He has made similar claims repeatedly in recent months — telling a rally in Qasur in July that the entire Baloch region had slipped out of the government's control, and rebuking army chief Field Marshal Asim Munir directly. In January 2025, he told the National Assembly that in parts of Balochistan the national anthem could not be sung, nor the flag raised, in some schools, for fear of attack.

## The backdrop

The remarks come amid one of the deadliest phases of Pakistan's twin insurgencies. In early July 2026, coordinated attacks across Balochistan killed at least 38 security personnel; in May, an assault on Bannu killed 21 police and Frontier Corps personnel; and the military has been running intensified intelligence-based operations — under Operation Radd-ul-Fitna and the Azm-e-Istehkam campaign — across both provinces.

Fazlur Rehman rejected the army chief's appeal for civilians to take up arms alongside the military, arguing that protecting citizens is the state's own constitutional responsibility, not one to be transferred to ordinary people. He urged a broad national political dialogue rather than reliance on force, and said Pakistan's crises could not be resolved by coercion alone.

## The official position

The government and military reject the "collapse" framing. Military statements point to continuing operations across KP and Balochistan — with claims of dozens of militants killed and weapons and bomb-making material recovered — as evidence that the state is actively confronting the militancy rather than ceding ground. The gap between that account and Fazlur Rehman's is, in part, a dispute over what "control" means: the ability to mount operations, or the ability to guarantee ordinary people safety in their own districts.

Whatever the framing, the underlying picture he describes — contested control, shuttered businesses, migrating families, and police and civilians caught between militants and the state — is not one that the year's casualty figures contradict.
