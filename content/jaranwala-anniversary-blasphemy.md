Three years ago, on 16 August 2023, thousands of men swept through the Christian neighbourhoods of Jaranwala, in Pakistan's Faisalabad district, burning churches and looting homes after two local Christians were falsely accused of desecrating the Quran. No one was killed, but the day left a lasting mark: it was one of the most destructive assaults on a religious minority in Pakistan's recent history, and three years on, almost no one has been held to account. The anniversary is a moment to look at both what happened — and at how the machinery behind such violence has since shifted.

## What happened at Jaranwala

On the morning of 16 August 2023, rumours spread — amplified by announcements from mosque loudspeakers — that a Christian man had torn pages from a Quran. A mob, including supporters of the far-right Tehreek-e-Labbaik Pakistan (TLP), gathered and moved through Christian areas on the edge of Faisalabad.

Estimates of the damage vary: reporting cited between roughly 20 and 26 churches burned or vandalised and around 80 to 100 homes damaged, while some 400 Christian families fled. Police later concluded the accusation had been fabricated — that Quran pages had been planted to implicate two men over a personal dispute.

More than 100 people were arrested in the immediate aftermath, a figure that later rose to around 200; paramilitary Rangers were deployed and public gatherings banned for a week. Authorities repaired most of the churches and paid compensation to affected families. But three years later, prosecutions of those who led the mob have largely stalled, and many who fled remain afraid to return.

## A long pattern

Jaranwala was not an aberration. In 2009, a mob in Gojra, also in Punjab, burned dozens of Christian homes and killed six people after a blasphemy accusation. In 2012, a cleric near Islamabad accused Rimsha Masih — a Christian girl of about 14 with Down syndrome — of burning Quran pages; the resulting mob threat forced hundreds of Christian families to flee before the cleric himself was found to have planted the evidence.

The pattern is consistent: an accusation, often unproven or fabricated, spreads faster than any court can test it; a crowd forms; and homes, churches, and lives are destroyed before the state intervenes — if it intervenes at all. Under Pakistan's blasphemy laws, insulting Islam can carry the death penalty. No one has ever been executed by the state for blasphemy, but the accusation alone has repeatedly proved lethal at the hands of mobs.

## The new frontier: accusation as a business

Since Jaranwala, rights monitors say the mechanics of accusation have moved online — and have been industrialised. In January 2024, a Punjab police Special Branch report titled "The Blasphemy Business" described an organised network working with officials of the Federal Investigation Agency (FIA) to entrap people through social media and messaging apps, then blackmail and extort them. The report found the group responsible for a large share of the FIA's online blasphemy cases since 2021, and that it included members of religious organisations, among them the TLP.

The National Commission for Human Rights (NCHR) documented how young men were lured — often by operatives posing as women — into sharing or being framed with "blasphemous" material online, then arrested, at times by private individuals rather than police, with statements extracted under duress. Rights lawyers say hundreds of families have petitioned the courts; the Islamabad High Court ordered an inquiry into the racket, though advocates report the effort has since stalled. The Centre for Social Justice recorded at least 344 blasphemy accusations in 2024 alone — and, tellingly, most of the accused that year were themselves Muslims, alongside Ahmadis, Hindus, and Christians. The law is a weapon that can be turned on almost anyone.

## The toll continues

The violence has not stopped. In May 2024, a mob in Sargodha attacked an elderly Christian man over an alleged Quran desecration, beating him and setting property alight; he later died of his injuries. In September 2024, Shagufta Kiran, a Christian mother of four, was sentenced to death over material she was accused of sharing in a WhatsApp group. There have been acquittals too — Christian twin brothers were cleared in January 2025 after the case against them collapsed — but each acquittal follows months or years of imprisonment, fear, and displacement.

## An accountability gap

What ties Jaranwala to the cases that followed is not only the violence but the impunity. Mobs disperse, footage circulates, commissions are announced — and prosecutions fade. Human Rights Watch, in a 2025 report on the blackmail and extortion built around these laws, and Pakistani rights bodies alike have urged that accusations be tested by courts rather than crowds, and that those who orchestrate false cases and mob attacks be held to account. Three years after Jaranwala, that remains the unmet demand.
