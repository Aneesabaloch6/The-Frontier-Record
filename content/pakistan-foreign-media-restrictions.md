Pakistan's Ministry of Information and Broadcasting has issued a new set of rules — the Foreign Media Facilitation Guidelines, 2026 — requiring foreign correspondents, and Pakistani nationals who work for international outlets, to obtain government permission before reporting from almost anywhere in the country. The move has drawn condemnation from press-freedom groups, rights bodies, and international broadcasters.

## What the rules require

Under the guidelines, anyone reporting for a foreign media organisation — including Pakistani employees, contributors, freelancers, and fixers — must secure a No Objection Certificate (NOC) from the ministry before working outside Islamabad, Karachi, and Lahore. The policy sets no limit on how long an NOC can take to approve; foreign journalists already report waiting months, or receiving no answer at all.

The guidelines also allow accreditation to be revoked for "acts against ideology, sovereignty, security or public order" — wording critics call dangerously vague and open to abuse. Because on-the-ground reporting in a country of some 240 million people would now depend on official permission and timing, journalist groups warn the rules place independent coverage at the discretion of the state.

## The backdrop: Kashmir, blackouts, and a blocked broadcaster

The timing is not incidental. The guidelines landed during the deadliest unrest in years in Pakistan-administered Kashmir, where the security response to protests has left dozens dead. In the run-up, authorities blocked several news websites, imposed internet and mobile blackouts, and restricted access to Al Jazeera's website after accusing the broadcaster of "yellow journalism." Foreign media will now need permission even to travel to the region.

## A wave of condemnation

The reaction was swift and broad:

- A coalition of 13 local and international civil society organisations, **led by Amnesty International**, wrote an open letter to Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and senior ministers urging an end to the escalating restrictions, and called for the guidelines to be withdrawn and the Al Jazeera block reversed.
- The **BBC** said it was "extremely concerned," stressing that its journalists — including local staff based in Pakistan — must be able to travel across the country to cover events and hear ordinary citizens.
- The **Committee to Protect Journalists (CPJ)** called the rules draconian and an appalling blow to press freedom, warning they were likely to censor coverage of the Kashmir unrest, and urged their withdrawal.
- The **Human Rights Commission of Pakistan (HRCP)** said it was deeply concerned and warned the measures would institutionalise bureaucratic control over news-gathering.
- The **Pakistan Federal Union of Journalists (PFUJ)** condemned the guidelines as "an attack on freedom of expression" and warned of protest if they were not withdrawn.

## The government's position

The Ministry of Information rejected the criticism, saying the guidelines are "not intended to restrict foreign media" but to provide a clear, transparent, and accountable framework for the registration, accreditation, and facilitation of foreign media organisations. It said the rules distinguish between registering media personnel and seeking clearance for reporting trips outside the three cities, and that they do not amount to a blanket restriction on individual movement.

## A shrinking space

Pakistan already ranks 153rd of 180 countries on the World Press Freedom Index. Press-freedom advocates argue that channelling all reporting through official permission — with no deadline for approval and vague grounds for stripping accreditation — will chill independent coverage precisely where scrutiny is most needed.

A government confident in its own conduct, the critics note, has little to fear from journalists. The tighter the restrictions on who may report, and from where, the sharper the question of what is being kept out of view.
